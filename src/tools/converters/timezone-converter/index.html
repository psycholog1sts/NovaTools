What Time Zone Converter Does and Why It Matters

Time Zone Converter shows the current time in its listed IANA zones and converts a specific local date and time from one listed zone into another — the calculation people usually do in their head and get wrong once daylight saving or an odd UTC offset (like UTC+5:30) is involved.

This matters most for scheduling across locations: setting up a call with people in different countries, checking whether a deadline in another timezone has already passed, or working out what time a live event airs locally. Manual timezone math is a common source of scheduling mistakes, particularly because not every region observes daylight saving time, and the ones that do don't all switch on the same date.

How to Use Time Zone Converter

  1. Select the source timezone and the time you want to convert.
  2. Select the target timezone.
  3. The converted time is shown immediately.
  4. Use the current-time view to check what time it is right now in any listed timezone.

What It Accounts For

  • Date-specific UTC offsets from the browser's IANA/Intl timezone data, including non-hour offsets such as India's UTC+5:30
  • Daylight-saving transitions represented by that browser timezone database for the selected date
  • Explicit rejection of local wall times that do not exist during a DST spring gap or occur twice during a DST fall-back fold

Privacy and Security

Times and timezones you select are used only to compute the result shown and aren't sent anywhere or stored.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does it account for daylight saving time automatically?

For the listed IANA zones, the converter uses the browser's Intl timezone database for the selected date. It rejects ambiguous and nonexistent local times instead of guessing. Historical and future accuracy is therefore limited by the timezone data shipped with the browser, and future legislation cannot be predicted.

Why is the offset for some countries not a whole number of hours?

A handful of regions (India, parts of Australia, Iran, and others) use a half-hour or 45-minute offset from UTC rather than a whole hour. This is a real, standard part of their timezone definition, not a rounding artifact.

What timezone should I use for a fully remote/global meeting invite?

UTC is the safest common reference for a meeting spanning many timezones, since it doesn't shift with anyone's local daylight saving changes — convert UTC to each attendee's local time individually rather than picking one attendee's timezone as the base.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does daylight saving time affect conversions?

For listed IANA zones, date-specific offsets come from the browser's Intl timezone data. Ambiguous fall-back times and nonexistent spring-gap times are reported instead of silently choosing an offset.

What is UTC?

UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) is the primary time standard by which the world regulates clocks and time. It does not observe daylight saving time and serves as a reference point for all other time zones.

Can I convert dates in the past or future?

You can enter past or future dates supported by the browser. Results use that browser's installed IANA timezone data; they do not guarantee future legislative changes or timezone history missing from the runtime.

Is my data stored?

No. All conversions happen locally in your browser. No data is transmitted to our servers or stored anywhere.

Why is the time difference not a whole number of hours?

Some time zones use offsets that include minutes (e.g., India is UTC+5:30, Nepal is UTC+5:45). The listed zones use their date-specific offsets from the browser timezone database, including supported half-hour offsets.
